# Projection drivers for single-game NBA player points, rebounds, and assists

Researcher notes compiled 2026-10-29. Labels on each finding: [Measured] = a quantitative result reported from data; [Rule of thumb] = a practitioner heuristic with no published validation; [Claim] = a qualitative or unvalidated statement; [Snippet] = taken from a search-result summary because the full text could not be opened (verify before relying on it); [Pre-2018] = older work, era context differs (pace and three-point rates were lower).

## 1. How should minutes be projected, and what error do good minutes projections achieve? (Includes blowouts, foul trouble, back-to-backs, rest, injury returns, and minutes restrictions)

### Takeaway
Minutes are the largest and least predictable input. A player's season-to-date minutes average stabilizes almost immediately (padding of 1.52 games), but single-game minutes errors stay large. The only public game-level benchmark found is a 2019-20 student LightGBM model: MSE 38.24 (RMSE about 6.2 minutes) against 50.24 (about 7.1 minutes) for a 5-game average. DARKO, a public projection system, reports that commercial DFS sites beat it only on minutes, which points to news-driven inputs (injury report, confirmed lineups, coach statements) as the main source of minutes accuracy. Blowout, foul-trouble, and minutes-restriction adjustments used in practice are rules of thumb, not validated effects.

### Cited Findings

#### How public and commercial systems structure the minutes projection
- [Measured] DARKO (Kostya Medvedovsky's public daily projection system) projects playing time as separate components, each with its own exponential-decay half-life, stated in games for a 31-year-old and adjusted by age: "Starting role" about 1 game, "Minutes role" about 2 games, "Playing at all" about 3 games ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Claim] DARKO defines its minutes forecast as "a player's minutes a game when he plays, from his recent playing time and role," and a player ruled out on the official injury report "still shows his minutes when healthy," so availability is handled separately from minutes-when-active ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Measured, no error figures published] DARKO states "Minutes are the hardest thing any projection system forecasts." In its comparison against two unnamed DFS sites (seasons not stated), DARKO beat both sites on points, rebounds, assists, blocks, turnovers, and threes made; "The only stat where DARKO lost was in minutes projections" ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Measured] Season-to-date minutes per game need a padding of only 1.52 games toward the league average (20.315 MPG) to best predict rest-of-season minutes. The author says minutes are "extremely stable (duh), since they're almost entirely a coach's decision." Method: padding chosen by differential evolution to predict rest-of-season performance after every game of every season since 2001, about 750K rows (Medvedovsky, August 6, 2020) ([kmedved.com](https://kmedved.com/2020/08/06/nba-stabilization-rates-and-the-padding-approach/)).
- [Rule of thumb] RotoGrinders DFS lesson (Derek Farnsworth, updated 2024-04-09): baseline minutes = (season MPG x 0.75) + (last-5-games MPG x 0.25); a team has 240 minutes to allocate; role changes are identified by comparing recent minutes, usage, and production with season averages; bench minute spikes from blowouts are not role changes; injury reallocation is "more of an art than a science" ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/fantasy/lessons/accurately-predicting-minutes-nba-dfs)).
- [Claim, Snippet] NBA starting lineups are typically confirmed about 30 minutes before tip-off ([Fantasy Team Advice](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nba/starting-lineups)). Razzball says its minutes are "automatically reallocated upon any lineup/injury-related changes" ([Razzball](https://basketball.razzball.com/lineups/)).
- [Claim, Snippet] Unabated's NBA-only "market-based prop projections" blend median prop lines across sportsbooks and work the distribution backward to recover the implied mean ([Unabated](https://unabated.com/post/introducing-market-based-prop-projections)).
- [Claim, Snippet] A SportsBookReview handicapper forum thread argues minute redistribution after injuries is too noisy to predict well because coaches' replacement plans vary ([SBR forum](https://www.sportsbookreview.com/forum/handicapper-think-tank/594104-nba-modeling-injuries)).

#### Accuracy benchmarks
- [Measured, student project] UBC Master of Data Science project (2019-20 cohort) predicting next-game minutes with LightGBM on the Kaggle "NBA Enhanced Box Score and Standings" dataset (Paul Rossotti; 2012 to 2018; 151,493 player-game rows): test MSE 38.24, R^2 0.65, versus MSE 50.24, R^2 0.55 for a 5-game-average baseline. RMSE and MAE not reported; feature list not shown ([GitHub: UBC-MDS NBA-Minutes-Predictor](https://github.com/UBC-MDS/NBA-Minutes-Predictor)).
- [Measured; effect sizes are Snippet] Köhler, Tegtmeier and Döpke, "Are they worth it? An evaluation of predictions for NBA 'Fantasy Sports'" (Journal of Economics and Finance 48(1), 2024) assembled 1,658 projections from four providers collected in February 2022; regression-based efficiency tests found the forecasts "inefficient and, in some cases, even biased" ([abstract via Bohrium](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/9M937040/Lars_Tegtmeier); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-023-09646-7)). A search summary of the article states that professional forecasts reduce the errors of a naive forecast (the athlete's previous score) by less than 10% and that errors are slightly more than half the standard deviation of actual outcomes. Full text not accessed. These are fantasy points, not minutes.
- [Unreviewed, Snippet] A personal GitHub research log (October 28, 2026) backtesting bench "step-in" minutes with NBA.com historical daily lineups (2023-24 to 2025-26) reports errors of 5.67 and 5.63 minutes for a model using confirmed starters versus 6.28 and 6.00 for its prior version; the error metric definition was not visible ([GitHub: Eth91 PR #114](https://github.com/Eth91/tennis-odds-collector/pull/114)).

#### Blowouts and the point spread
- [Rule of thumb] RotoGrinders: start reducing projected minutes at a 7-point spread, 1.5% per additional point (8-point spread x 0.985; 10-point x 0.955; 14-point x 0.895); avoid players on the expected losing side when the spread is at least 8, "although most people like to use 10-point spreads." The article's worked example for 14 points has an arithmetic typo, but the 89.5% result is correct ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/fantasy/lessons/accurately-predicting-minutes-nba-dfs)).
- [Rule of thumb, Snippet] A 2026 betting guide treats spreads of 8 to 12 points as moderate blowout risk and 13+ as high, with starters losing about 3 to 5 minutes in big-spread games and bench players gaining garbage time ([Land Your Bets](https://landyourbets.com/how-to-make-nba-minutes-projections)).
- [Claim, Snippet] The Wager Theorem recommends a probability-weighted mix of a full-game scenario and a blowout scenario and notes the spread gives expected margin, not blowout probability ([Wager Theorem: risk adjustment](https://thewagertheorem.com/nba-player-props-risk-adjustment/); [Wager Theorem: garbage time](https://thewagertheorem.com/nba-player-props-garbage-time/)).
- [Rule of thumb] Stokastic (August 17, 2026) uses a double-digit spread, "roughly 10 points or more," as a blowout-risk threshold ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/dfs-strategy/nba-pickem-correlation-strategy)).

#### Foul trouble
- [Measured, Pre-2018] Maymin, Maymin and Shen, "How Much Trouble Is Early Foul Trouble? Strategically Idling Resources in the NBA" (SSRN January 2011; International Journal of Sport Finance 2012, pp. 324-339), play-by-play for starters on all 30 teams (summaries disagree on the period: 2006 to 2009 per ESPN TrueHoop, 2006-07 per another report). Teams performed significantly worse when a starter in foul trouble stayed in, strongest in the third quarter; proposed "Q+1" rule (bench a starter who has one more foul than the current quarter number). An ESPN summary puts the first-half benefit of benching at 5 to 10 percent of win probability. A reported figure that coaches bench players in foul trouble about 67 percent of the time could not be verified against the paper ([ESPN TrueHoop](https://www.espn.com/blog/truehoop/post/_/id/25832/how-much-trouble-is-early-foul-trouble); [FiT Publishing](https://fitpublishing.com/node/1046); [NYU Engineering](https://engineering.nyu.edu/news/rethinking-foul-trouble)).
- [Measured] Personal fouls per 100 possessions: padding 218.92 possessions toward a league average of 4.475 (2001 onward data), compared with 60.52 for rebounds per 100 ([kmedved.com](https://kmedved.com/2020/08/06/nba-stabilization-rates-and-the-padding-approach/)).

#### Back-to-backs and rest
- [Measured, team-level, one season] NBAAnalytic (published June 4, 2026; updated September 25, 2026; data from NBA.com via nba_api) compared 441 second nights of back-to-backs with 1,989 games after 1+ days off in 2025-26, no travel or opponent adjustment: win% 48.3 vs 50.4; points per game 114.8 vs 115.7; FG% 47.0 vs 47.2; average margin -1.17 vs +0.27. No player-level effects reported; its "roughly 96% of himself" line for stars is derived from team win rates, not player data ([NBAAnalytic](https://nbaanalytic.com/articles/rest-back-to-backs-load-management.html)).
- [Measured, team-level] Aicardi and Bailey, "Running on Empty: How Back-to-Backs Impact Pace and the Four Factors of Basketball Success" (Cureus, 2026): over 20,000 games (2013-14 to 2023-24 per a search summary). Second nights of back-to-backs significantly reduce offensive efficiency, rebounding rates, and pace; teams grab fewer offensive rebounds and allow more second-chance opportunities; effects "tend to be small, around 0.5-2%" ([Economist Writing Every Day, July 9, 2026](https://economistwritingeveryday.com/2026/07/09/do-nba-teams-play-worse-in-back-to-back-games/); [Cureus listing](https://www.cureusjournals.com/articles/12833-running-on-empty-how-back-to-backs-impact-pace-and-the-four-factors-of-basketball-success)).
- [Measured, team-level] Esteves, Mikolajec, Schelling and Sampaio (European Journal of Sport Science 21(1):26-35; online 2020) analyzed all teams' 82 games in 2016-17 by rest cycle (back-to-back, 1 day, 2 days, 3+ days): the likelihood of winning increased significantly from back-to-back to one day of rest, and shooting-efficacy statistics discriminated between congestion cycles ([LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4069360?lng=en)).
- [Claim] DARKO: "players perform worse on the road or on the second night of a back-to-back," and DARKO "accounts for these effects on a component-by-component level, and the adjustments themselves update daily." Magnitudes not published ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).

#### Injury returns and minutes restrictions
- [Claim, example] Victor Wembanyama played 21 minutes in his first game back from a calf strain under a coach-confirmed minutes restriction, against a season average of just under 34 minutes, with a gradual ramp-up expected ([Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/victor-wembanyama-minutes-restriction-know-090001689.html)).
- [Rule of thumb, Snippet] Restrictions are often not on the injury report. Signals: the coach's pregame press conference (via beat reporters), minutes in the last 3 or 4 games compared with the usual average, shootaround reports. Example: a player projected for 34 minutes who plays 24 loses nearly a third of his scoring chances ([BasketUSA guide](https://www.basketusa.com/paris-sportifs/guides/nba-injury-report/)).
- [Measured, small sample] Statathlon (May 23, 2025): 20 cases (19 players) from the past 10 seasons with absences of at least 15 games or one month, at least 8 games before and after, 20+ MPG. Per-36 points (t = -1.3934, p = 0.1815), rebounds (t = 0.4301, p = 0.6725), and assists (t = -0.7102, p = 0.4872) did not differ significantly after return. The article's conclusion ("underperform in the first few games") contradicts its own results section ("modest increase") ([Statathlon](https://statathlon.com/evaluating-the-effect-of-mid-season-absences/)).

### Inferences
- Structure the minutes model as P(active) x P(start) x E[minutes | active, role], then apply a game-script mixture (normal game vs blowout vs foul trouble vs overtime). This mirrors DARKO's separate "playing at all," "starting role," and "minutes role" components. Their 1 to 3 game half-lives mean the last few games and the news feed should dominate; the RotoGrinders 75/25 season/last-5 blend is a slower weighting than DARKO's.
- An RMSE of about 6.2 minutes (square root of 38.24) on a 32-minute projection is about 19% of the expected count for every stat that scales with minutes. That is larger than most single opponent, venue, or rest adjustments documented in Section 3 (a few percent each; the arena scorekeeper effect on assists is the main exception). If errors were normal, MAE would be about 0.8 x RMSE, roughly 5 minutes; real minutes errors are heavy-tailed (injury exits, blowouts, ejections), so this conversion is rough.
- The spread rules of thumb roughly agree with each other: under the RotoGrinders formula a 12-point spread cuts 7.5%, about 2.5 minutes for a 34-minute starter, near the low end of the 3 to 5 minute rule.
- For double-double probabilities the full minutes distribution matters, not only its mean: a blowout or foul-trouble scenario truncates points, rebounds, and assists at the same time, which is a main source of positive dependence among them.
- Team-level back-to-back effects are about 1% on points (0.9 points per game in 2025-26; 0.5 to 2% across 2013-14 to 2023-24). For an individual prop, the larger back-to-back risk is likely availability (rest decisions) and minutes, but no player-level estimate was found.
- Statathlon's null result on per-36 rates after long absences (small n) is consistent with treating a returning player's per-minute rates as roughly unchanged and putting the adjustment into minutes.

### Gaps
- No published MAE for commercial NBA minutes projections (RotoWire, ETR, SaberSim, Basketball Monster, Hashtag Basketball were not found to publish accuracy). DARKO's DFS comparison gives no numbers.
- The FiveThirtyEight NBA methodology URL now redirects to abcnews.com/politics and the Wayback Machine could not be fetched from this environment, so 538's depth-chart playing-time method could not be reviewed.
- No measured curve linking point spread to starter minutes; no measured overtime frequency or overtime minutes adjustment; no player-level back-to-back minutes effect; no game-level foul-trouble minutes loss by position; no systematic data on minutes in the first games back from injury. Rufus Peabody and Pinnacle material on minutes was not found.

## 2. How stable are per-minute rebound and assist rates, and what shrinkage or recency weighting works best?

### Takeaway
Per-possession rebound and assist rates stabilize quickly against a league-average prior: padding of 60.52 possessions for rebounds per 100 and 55.57 for assists per 100 (about one game of a starter's court time), 72.41 for usage, 98.55 for offensive rebound percentage, and 108.26 for defensive rebound percentage. Year-over-year correlations are high (total rebound% 0.950, offensive 0.933, defensive 0.918, assist% 0.906, usage 0.824 among players who changed roles, 1978 to 2011 data). DARKO's optimized exponential-decay half-lives are long for rates (assist rate about 24 games, points about 31, defensive rebounds about 46, offensive rebound rate about 54) and short for usage (about 17) and very short for role and minutes (1 to 3 games). The practical recipe supported by these sources: long exponential windows for rates, short windows for minutes, plus Marcel-style or padding-style shrinkage to a prior.

#### Year-over-year reliability of REB%, AST%, USG%
- [Measured, Pre-2018] Neil Paine, "Which Rate Stats Correlate More With a Player's Role Than His Skill?" (Basketball-Reference blog, July 9, 2011). Sample: players who moved to a new role between seasons, at least 500 minutes in both seasons, aged 24 to 34, team-seasons since 1978; 1,866 player-seasons. Correlation of Year Y rate with the player's own Year Y-1 rate / with the Year Y-1 rate of the player whose role he took: Usage% 0.824 / 0.032; OReb% 0.933 / 0.648; DReb% 0.918 / 0.674; Tot Reb% 0.950 / 0.718; Ast% 0.906 / 0.681; Blk% 0.912 / 0.457; Stl% 0.793 / 0.303; Tov% 0.726 / 0.257 ([Basketball-Reference blog](https://www.basketball-reference.com/blog/index1540.html?p=9776)).
- [Measured, Pre-2018] Neil Paine (2010): among players aged 24 to 34 with at least 1,000 minutes in back-to-back seasons since 1974, the top quartile by absolute usage change (1,036 players, "definitely changed roles"): offensive rebounding % and assist rate were the most consistent rate stats, "almost completely independent of a player's role"; true shooting % was the least consistent ([Neil Paine Substack repost](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/which-offensive-rate-stats-stay-the)).
- [Measured, context] Model-based rebounding impact (not raw rates) is far noisier: a 2023 tracking-data analysis reports single-year ridge-regressed offensive rebounding impact year-over-year R^2 of 0.16 (tracking-based model 0.77) and defensive about 0.1 (tracking-based 0.60 to 0.64) ([Jumpshot in the Dark](https://jumpshotinthedark.com/2023/07/03/quantifying-the-nbas-most-impactful-rebounders-using-tracking-data/)).

#### Recency weighting and shrinkage in public systems
- [Measured] DARKO weights each past game by beta^t, with beta between 0 and 1 and t the number of days since the game; beta is fit separately for each stat with a differential-evolution optimizer to best predict future results. Published half-lives (games, for a 31-year-old, age-adjusted; 16 of 36 stats shown): starting role about 1; minutes role about 2; playing at all about 3; share of shots from three about 13; usage about 17; assist rate about 24; points about 31; blocks about 41; defensive rebounds about 46; offensive rebound rate about 54; steal rate about 75; turnovers about 1.1 seasons; free-throw % about 1.4 seasons; three-point % about 1.5 seasons; % at the rim about 2.5 seasons; corner three % about 8 seasons ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Claim] DARKO combines the exponential-decay projections with a "modified Kalman filter" through a gradient-boosted decision tree; pulls every player back toward average between seasons; gives players who change teams a higher "learning rate"; fits a separate aging curve per stat; models seasonality per component (including a temporary flattening of offensive rate growth around the All-Star break) and interactions between box-score components. Its ratings use 787,782 player games since 1996-97 ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Claim] In a HoopsHype survey of NBA team staff cited by DARKO, 8 of 29 respondents named DPM their preferred public all-in-one metric and 10 more said they trusted it ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Measured method, no accuracy figures] Basketball-Reference's Simple Projection System (a Marcel adaptation): season weights 6 (most recent), 3, 1; add 1,000 minutes of league-average production to both the weighted stat and weighted minutes; projected per-36 rate = (weighted stat + regressed league-average stat) / (weighted minutes + 1,000) x 36; age adjustment (28 - age) x 0.004 under 28 and (28 - age) x 0.002 over 28 (sign reversed for misses, turnovers, fouls). The page reports no accuracy figures ([Basketball-Reference SPS](https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/projections.html)).
- [Rule of thumb, unvalidated] An open-source prop tool shrinks per-minute points, rebounds, and assists toward a baseline with n/(n+12) and weights 70% season, 20% last 10, 10% last 5; its README reports no backtest results ([GitHub: Propdesk](https://github.com/tobyweekes-ctrl/Propdesk)).
- [Claim] A 2026 guest post on estimating true skill (Jeremias Engelmann) describes ridge/lasso/elastic-net penalized regression toward league average, Monte Carlo weighting of a league-wide prior histogram by the binomial likelihood, and Bayesian binomial models in PyMC, as alternatives to fixed padding ([5x5 | Royce Webb](https://www.roycewebb.com/p/how-to-accurately-predict-nba-player)).

### Inferences
- Padding converts directly to an empirical-Bayes weight: weight on the player's own data = n / (n + k). At a league pace of about 99 to 100 possessions per 48 minutes (Stokastic, 2026), a player sees about 2.07 team possessions per minute, so k = 60.52 possessions for rebounds per 100 is about 29 minutes of court time, 55.57 for assists about 27 minutes, 72.41 for usage about 35 minutes, and 108.26 for DRB% about 52 minutes. After 10 games at 31 minutes (about 640 possessions) the weight on the player's own rebound rate is about 0.91 and on his DRB% about 0.86.
- These small padding values reflect a league-wide prior, where between-player spread (centers vs guards) is large. With a position-specific or prior-season prior, the talent variance around the prior is smaller, so the appropriate padding would be larger. A pipeline that shrinks toward the player's own multi-season history (SPS-style 6/3/1) needs a different, larger k, which would have to be fit on its own data.
- DARKO half-lives converted to per-game decay factors (0.5^(1/h)): assist rate 0.9715 (effective sample size (1+lambda)/(1-lambda) about 69 games); points 0.9779 (about 90 games); defensive rebounds 0.9851 (about 133 games); offensive rebound rate 0.9872 (about 156 games); usage 0.9601 (about 49 games); minutes role 0.7071 (about 6 games). DARKO's t is in days, so these game-based conversions are approximate.
- Together these say rebound and assist talent changes slowly (long half-lives, r about 0.9 year to year) while minutes and role change fast. A next-game model should weight rates over roughly a season or more with exponential decay and weight minutes over the last few games plus news.
- Paine's role correlations imply that when a player moves into a new role (for example a backup center promoted to starter), his rebounding and assist rates partly move toward the role's norm (r 0.65 to 0.72 with the predecessor) while usage stays with the player (r 0.03 with the predecessor). Part of that role correlation may reflect replacements being similar players, so this is suggestive, not causal.

### Gaps
- No 2020s year-over-year reliability table for REB%, AST%, USG% was found; Paine's data run 1978 to 2011.
- Medvedovsky's padding is fit to a rest-of-season target; next-game-optimized padding was not published. The meaning of the "ast_avail" denominator is not explained in the text.
- Only 16 of DARKO's 36 half-lives are visible on the About page; exact beta values and validation errors are not published.
- No published comparison of half-life choices (or exponential decay vs season average) on next-game rebound or assist MAE was found. Hashtag Basketball, Basketball Monster, Cleaning the Glass, and Nylon Calculus material on recency weighting was not found.

## 3. How much do opponent pace and opponent rebounding or assist allowances move an individual's expected rebounds and assists? (Also home vs away, altitude, and schedule spots)

### Takeaway
Opponent pace is reliable early (team pace reaches r^2 = 0.5 with its season-end value after 4 games) and scales all counting stats roughly proportionally; the common pace formulas give nearly identical answers. I found no public, quantified effect size for opponent rebounding or assist allowances on an individual's rebounds or assists; the one practitioner who reports backtesting defense-vs-position (DvP) calls it largely noise. Home court: in 2019-20 before COVID, home teams averaged 1.08 more defensive rebounds and 1.02 more assists per game (effect size 0.21 each), and the gap vanished in 2020-21 no-fan games; arena scorekeepers shift recorded assists by up to 5.76 per game. Denver shows the largest home edge at team level. Back-to-back effects at team level are about 0.5 to 2%.

### Cited Findings

#### Pace
- [Claim, Snippet] Projected game pace methods: the simple average of the two teams' paces as a baseline, and the RotoAcademy deviation method (add both teams' deviations from league average to the league average; a 102-pace team vs a 96-pace team in a 98 league projects to about 100 possessions vs 99 for the plain average) ([RotoGrinders RotoAcademy](https://rotogrinders.com/articles/rotoacademy-preview-predicting-pace-1570467)).
- [Measured] Team stats from the first X games vs season-end values, games needed for r^2 = 0.5 (Krishna Narsu, Nylon Calculus, December 21, 2017; previous four seasons): Pace 4; 3PAr 4; FTR 5; AST% 6; Opp. 3PAr 7; TOV% 10; ORB% 11; 2PT% 11; TS% 12; eFG% 12; Off Rtg 13; Opp. eFG% 14; Opp. FTR 14; DRB% 15; REB% 15; Opp. TOV% 15; FT% 16; Def Rtg 16; Opp. 2PT% 18; 3PT% 26; Opp. 3PT% 33 ([FanSided/Nylon Calculus](https://fansided.com/2017/12/21/nylon-calculus-team-stats-noise-stabilization-thunder/)).
- [Claim] League-average pace is roughly 99 to 100 possessions per 48 minutes (Stokastic, August 17, 2026) ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/dfs-strategy/nba-pickem-correlation-strategy)).

#### Opponent rebounding and assist allowances, defense vs position
- [Claim, Snippet] Fantasy Team Advice says it does not use DvP in its DFS projections because its backtests found DvP "mostly noise and not overly predictive," and it favors pace, team defensive rating, usage, and recent form; no numbers published ([Fantasy Team Advice DvP](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nba/dvp)).
- [Claim, Snippet] NBAstuffer says its refined DvP variant correlates better than traditional DvP, without published validation ([NBAstuffer](https://www.nbastuffer.com/analytics101/defense-vs-position/)).
- [Claim] DARKO's projections "account for who each team is playing on a given night," adjusting each stat for the opponent's projected influence; magnitudes not published ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Rule of thumb, unvalidated] An open-source prop tool multiplies projections by opponent defense against position (guards, forwards, centers) and caps a head-to-head factor at plus or minus 10% ([GitHub: Propdesk](https://github.com/tobyweekes-ctrl/Propdesk)).
- [Claim, not accessed] Kiriazis et al., "A Bayesian two-stage framework for lineup-independent assessment of individual rebounding ability in the NBA" (Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports, 2025) models individual rebounding ability and team rebound allocation; full text returned HTTP 405 ([De Gruyter DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/jqas-2023-0097)).

#### Home vs away
- [Measured] Lu, Zhang, Ding, Wang and Gomez (Frontiers in Psychology, November 22, 2022), team box scores, home vs away mean (SD): 2019-20 pre-COVID with fans (971 games): defensive rebounds 35.01 (5.09) vs 33.93 (5.39), p < 0.001, ES 0.21; offensive rebounds 10.28 vs 9.86, p < 0.01, ES 0.12; assists 24.72 (4.92) vs 23.70 (4.89), p < 0.001, ES 0.21; personal fouls 20.2 vs 20.57, p < 0.05. 2020-21 no-fan games (578 games): defensive rebounds 34.54 vs 34.56, p = 0.972; offensive rebounds 9.65 vs 10.05 (away higher), p < 0.05; assists 25.04 vs 24.82, p = 0.473 ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.951779/full)).
- [Measured] van Bommel and Bornn, "Adjusting for Scorekeeper Bias in NBA Box Scores" (Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 2017; 2015-16 season; tracking data for 1,227 of 1,230 games). Mean home-team "scorekeeper bonus" ranges from -3.44 assists per game (Utah Jazz scorekeeper) to +2.32 (New Orleans Pelicans), a 5.76-assist spread, against 22.05 assists per team-game. Of 82,493 potential assists, 54,111 (65.59%) were recorded as assists. Passer-position effects on recording probability: point guard +3.76 percentage points, center -4.01. Cross-season correlation of scorekeeper generosity coefficients: 0.887 (2014 vs 2015), 0.856 (2015 vs 2016), 0.776 (2014 vs 2016); of bias coefficients: 0.486, 0.631, 0.460 ([arXiv 1602.08754 via ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1602.08754)). Press coverage reported the Utah scorekeeper awarded 9.7 fewer assists per game than Atlanta's ([Daily Hive](https://dailyhive.com/toronto/sfu-study-nba-scorekeeper-bias)).
- [Measured] Bayesian analysis of home advantage (2021): NBA playoffs 2016 to 2019 pooled home advantage "tightly around 0.05" on the log scale (about 5%, roughly 5 points on about 107); the 2020 bubble estimate was lower (probability 0.999 that it is below 0.05; 0.17 that it is below 0) ([PMC8282683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8282683/)).
- [Measured, as summarized in a review] Systematic review of home advantage in elite basketball (2024): Cheng (2013 to 2018) home teams scored an additional 2.3 points; Ganz and Allsop (2014 to 2022) fans added 1.69 points to the home score vs no-fan games; Bustamante-Sanchez et al. (906 games, 2019-20) home teams superior in assists, rebounds, and shooting percentages (no numbers in the review); Ribeiro et al. (16,133 games, 2001 to 2014) "0.13 points per minute" more at home, which looks large relative to the other estimates and should be checked in the original ([PMC11503446](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11503446/)).
- [Measured, older data, working paper, Snippet] A Munich RePEc working paper (2014) table: home teams about 42.3 total rebounds (31.0 defensive) and 22.2 assists vs away 41.0 (30.1) and 20.6; home win rate about 60% ([MPRA 54579](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/54579/1/MPRA_paper_54579.pdf)).
- [Measured, Snippet; full text 403] "Not all players feel at home: exploring individual differences in the home advantage phenomenon across 23 NBA seasons" (International Journal of Performance Analysis in Sport, 2026): the largest player-level home-away differences clustered around rebounds, blocks, and two-point percentage; assists and turnovers formed a separate component ([Taylor and Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/24748668.2026.2635274)).
- [Measured, Snippet] A 2026 study of 114 NBA Finals games: home teams won 61.4%; rebounds, assists, and personal fouls were the only factors differing significantly, about four more rebounds and three more assists per game for home teams ([IJRSS](https://ijrss.org/index.php/ijrss/article/view/958)).

#### Altitude
- [Measured, team-level, via press] Lopez, Matthews and Baumer's model (covered in 2018) found Denver teams had the largest home advantage in the NBA, NFL, and MLB after accounting for team strength; the Nuggets could expect to win 66.1% of home games vs a typical 62% ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/joelrush/2018/09/12/study-affirms-altitude-boosts-denver-nuggets-home-advantage-but-do-other-factors-blunt-the-impact/); [MSU Denver RED](https://red.msudenver.edu/2018/advantage-altitude-exploring-the-largest-home-advantage-in-sports/)).
- [Measured, team-level, via press] Since 1999-00, Denver's net rating has been 8.6 points per 100 possessions better at home than on the road vs 6.0 for the average team; Utah, the only other arena above 1,200 feet, has the second-largest gap (Yahoo Sports, 2023) ([Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/altitude-gives-nuggets-rarefied-nba-095500917.html)).

#### Schedule spots and travel (see Section 1 for back-to-back numbers)
- [Measured, as summarized in a review] McHill and Chinoy (1,364 games, 2019-20): westward travel reduced winning percentage, shooting accuracy, and effort; Leota et al. (11,481 games, 2011 to 2021): eastward jet lag linked to fewer wins; Pradhan et al. (499 games, 2013 to 2019): eastward-traveling teams had more assists and fouls (no magnitudes in the review); Zhang et al. (1,214 games, 2021-22): schedule congestion and accumulated fatigue affect home advantage ([PMC11503446](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11503446/)).

### Inferences
- Pace acts as a multiplier on opportunities: in the RotoAcademy example the projected game is about 2% faster than league average, which raises every counting-stat expectation about 2% before other adjustments. A multiplicative form, (team pace x opponent pace) / league pace, gives 99.9 in the same example (my calculation; no authoritative source for this form was confirmed), so formula choice matters less than using current-season pace (reliable after about 4 games).
- Rebound opportunities are mechanical: a player's defensive rebound chances depend on the opponent's missed shots (opponent eFG%, free-throw rate, turnovers) and the opponent's offensive rebounding; offensive chances depend on his own team's misses and the opponent's DRB%. A transparent opponent adjustment for defensive rebounds is expected team defensive rebounds = projected possessions x opponent misses per possession x (1 - opponent OREB%), relative to league average, multiplied by the player's share of team defensive rebounds. Opponent DRB% and REB% need about 15 games to reach r^2 = 0.5, so early-season opponent factors should be shrunk to prior-season values.
- Assists need made baskets: a passer's assist expectation scales with his teammates' shooting against that opponent (opponent eFG% allowed) and with the arena scorekeeper.
- The 2019-20 home edges (about +3.2% defensive rebounds, +4.3% assists at team level) are a reasonable starting multiplier for player-level home/away splits when crowds are present; the no-fan season shows they are crowd-linked and should be re-estimated on 2023-24 to 2025-26 data before use.
- Arena assist factors look persistent (generosity coefficients correlate 0.78 to 0.89 across seasons), so a shrunk multi-season "assist park factor" by home arena is likely to carry signal for assist props. The home-bonus range (-3.44 to +2.32 per team-game on a 22.05 base) is about -16% to +11%.
- Altitude evidence is team-level only; any effect on visiting players' box scores likely shows up through fatigue (minutes, efficiency) rather than rebound or assist rates, and was not measured.

### Gaps
- No published, quantified effect of opponent DRB%, opponent rebounds allowed by position, or opponent assists allowed on an individual's rebounds or assists. FTA's "mostly noise" DvP finding has no published numbers.
- No player-level home/away effects for 2023-24 onward; the 2026 IJPAS player-level paper could not be opened.
- No player-level altitude effect; no quantified back-to-back or travel effect on individual rebounds or assists.
- Cleaning the Glass and Dunks and Threes opponent-adjustment material was not found or not accessible.

## 4. When a team's starting center or primary playmaker is out, how do teammates' rebounds and assists change on average?

### Takeaway
I found no league-wide published study giving the average change in teammates' rebounds and assists when a starting center or primary playmaker sits. The usable evidence is indirect. Rebounding has strong diminishing returns, especially on the defensive glass: older studies estimate a large share of a player's defensive rebounds would otherwise go to teammates, so a missing center's defensive rebounds are largely redistributed, mostly to other bigs. Usage follows the player rather than the role (r = 0.82 with own prior usage vs 0.03 with the predecessor), while rebound and assist rates are partly role-driven (r 0.65 to 0.72 with the predecessor). Practitioners rely on with-or-without-you (on/off) splits, with anecdotal usage spikes.

### Cited Findings

#### Rebounding redistribution (diminishing returns)
- [Measured, Pre-2018, Snippet; full page could not be opened] Phil Birnbaum (2011) estimated that teammates' rebounds fall as one player's rebounds rise, with roughly 70% of a player's rebounds hypothetically available to someone else on the team; in one season's data each extra center rebound came with about 0.61 fewer teammate rebounds (about 39% additive). His 2010-11 per-position estimates (with SE): PG 0.79 +/- 0.35; SG 0.92 +/- 0.21; SF 0.52 +/- 0.20; PF 0.94 +/- 0.13; C 0.77 +/- 0.14. The exact regression definitions could not be confirmed ([Sabermetric Research: do players steal rebounds](http://blog.philbirnbaum.com/2011/01/do-players-steal-rebounding.html); [Sabermetric Research: 2010-11 correlations](http://blog.philbirnbaum.com/2011/01/2010-11-nba-rebounding-correlations.html)).
- [Measured, Pre-2018, Snippet] Birnbaum (2008) found only a small diminishing-returns effect on offensive rebounds compared with defensive rebounds ([Sabermetric Research 2008](http://blog.philbirnbaum.com/2008/03/defensive-rebounding-stats-barely.html)).
- [Measured, forum, Snippet] An APBRmetrics lineup analysis reported each 1% of combined lineup defensive rebounding rate yielded only about 0.21% more actual team defensive rebounds ([APBRmetrics](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8844)).
- [Claim, Pre-2018] Nylon Calculus (2015): replacing a 10-rebound player with a 14-rebound player does not net four extra team rebounds, because part of the gain would have been collected anyway ([FanSided/Nylon Calculus](https://fansided.com/2015/09/09/bounded-by-reality-predicting-team-rebounding/)).

#### Role vs player: what transfers when a player steps into a new role
- [Measured, Pre-2018] Paine (2011), 1,866 role-changing player-seasons since 1978: correlation with the predecessor's prior-year rate in that role vs with the player's own prior-year rate: Usage% 0.032 vs 0.824; Tot Reb% 0.718 vs 0.950; DReb% 0.674 vs 0.918; OReb% 0.648 vs 0.933; Ast% 0.681 vs 0.906 ([Basketball-Reference blog](https://www.basketball-reference.com/blog/index1540.html?p=9776)).

#### Usage and playmaking redistribution (practitioner)
- [Claim, Snippet] Stokastic example: Caris LeVert posted 41.3% usage and 1.38 DraftKings points per minute in 261 minutes when the team's other creators were off the floor ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/dfs-strategy/nba-dfs-usage-rate-strategy)).
- [Claim, Pre-2018, Snippet] An older RotoGrinders piece reported Jrue Holiday and Anthony Davis each saw a "3-5%" bump in usage when teammates were absent (units as reported; percent vs percentage points not confirmed) ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/articles/nba-starting-five-courtiq-wednesday-march-16th-1219802)).
- [Claim, Snippet] Fantasy Team Advice recommends filtering lineup data with the star OFF to see which teammates gain usage, noting that standard usage rate is computed over all minutes regardless of who else is on the floor ([Fantasy Team Advice](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nba/usage-rate)). LineStar offers a "Usage Matrix" tool for the same purpose ([LineStar](https://linestar.gitbook.io/linestar-app/feature-guides/usage-matrix-nba)).
- [Claim, secondhand, Snippet] A study of nearly 30,000 NBA games, reported through a David Epstein column, found that when a star was injured, teams passed more and shot attempts became more evenly distributed, and strategy only partly reverted after the star returned; the original paper was not located ([Isegoria](https://www.isegoria.net/?p=54374)).
- [Claim] DARKO models interaction effects between box-score components and raises the learning rate for players who change teams ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).

### Inferences
- For a missing starting center, the mechanical approach consistent with the diminishing-returns work: take his projected defensive rebounds (rate x minutes), assume most would otherwise go to teammates (the roughly 0.5 to 0.9 coefficient range above), and allocate them in proportion to remaining players' rebound rates weighted toward the replacement big and the power forward. Offensive rebounds redistribute less, so do not reallocate them one-for-one.
- The replacement center's own rate should be his historical rate, nudged toward the role norm (Paine's role correlations of about 0.65 to 0.72), not set equal to the starter's rate.
- For a missing primary playmaker, assists do not transfer with the role as cleanly as minutes do. Usage is player-driven, so the vacated shots and creation go mainly to the remaining high-usage players; the secondary ball handler's assist rate rises but the team's total assists may fall if passing structure changes. Measure this per team with on/off splits rather than a league constant.
- On/off samples are small and noisy (a few hundred minutes), so shrink with-without differences toward zero or toward a league-wide redistribution model, using the padding logic from Section 2 (rebound and assist rates need roughly 55 to 110 possessions of padding against a league prior, more against a player prior).

### Gaps
- No league-wide, quantified study of teammates' rebound and assist changes when a starting center or primary playmaker is out; no 2018-or-later replication of rebounding diminishing returns; Birnbaum's coefficient definitions not confirmed.
- The original "star injury forces exploration" study was not located; the 30,000-game figure is secondhand.
- Kiriazis et al. (JQAS 2025) lineup-independent rebounding model could not be accessed; it may contain quantified teammate effects.
- No Unabated, Action Network, or Rufus Peabody material with numeric injury redistribution rules was found.

## 5. What distribution best fits single-game rebounds and assists, what is the within-game correlation among a player's points, rebounds, and assists, and how are joint probabilities (double-doubles, combined props) computed?

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed study that compares Poisson, negative binomial, and normal fits for single-game player rebounds or assists, and no published within-player correlation coefficients among points, rebounds, and assists. Indirect evidence: NBA team scoring is mildly overdispersed (dispersion statistic 1.50; negative binomial fits best by LOO, normal close behind, Poisson worst). Practitioners price counts with Poisson or simulation and stress that books set lines near the median while projections are means. For joint events, independence understates them: one unreviewed backtest reports independence under-rated triple-doubles by roughly half, and practitioner tools use Gaussian copulas or calibrated corrections.

### Cited Findings

#### Distributional evidence
- [Measured, team-level] Bayesian home-advantage study (2021): NBA point totals show potential overdispersion (dispersion statistic sigma_p = 1.50). LOO comparison: negative binomial -48964.8 (best), normal -48981.9 (dLOO 16.6, dSE 7.5), Poisson -49018.3 (dLOO 53.5, dSE 11.0); the authors describe "the Negative Binomial model starting to show small improvement for the slightly overdispersed NBA data" ([PMC8282683](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8282683/)).
- [Measured, team-level, Pre-2018, Snippet] "The Poisson model limits in NBA basketball: Complexity in team sports" (Physica A, 2016) argues a Poisson model is not compatible with the possible outcomes for points per possession and performs worse than a multinomial model in external validation based on box scores ([Universitat de Valencia repository](https://producciocientifica.uv.es/documentos/63cc8e46ab05b07b6665a2a1)).
- [Claim] Conway-Maxwell-Poisson distributions can be arbitrarily underdispersed when parametrized by the mean (Huang, 2020), giving an option if a stat turns out less variable than Poisson; no basketball rebound or assist application was found ([ar5iv 2011.07503](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.07503)).
- [Claim, practitioner example] A Patreon walkthrough prices a rebounds line by plugging an expected value (Bam Adebayo, mean 9.12 rebounds) into Excel's POISSON.DIST to get over/under probabilities ([Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/posts/98061524)).
- [Claim] Unabated (July 19, 2026): "most sportsbooks price props based on the Median while your projection is based on the Mean"; its props simulator runs a projection 10,000 times and reports both; it states NFL receptions are "priced according to a poisson distribution" and supports NBA points, rebounds, and assists without naming the NBA distribution ([Unabated props simulator](https://unabated.com/post/learn-about-the-props-simulator)).
- [Measured, Pre-2018, Snippet] A RotoGrinders analysis of 2012-13 box scores found steals and blocks, being rarer, have much larger relative variance than points and rebounds ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/lessons/nba-stat-variance-209254)).

#### Within-game correlation and joint probabilities
- [Claim] Betting guides describe correlations only qualitatively: Stokastic (August 17, 2026) labels pairings "Positive," "Negative," or "Shared downside" with written reasons and gives no coefficients, method, or sample ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/dfs-strategy/nba-pickem-correlation-strategy)); other guides note that pace and blowout risk move a player's counting stats together ([OddsIndex correlated parlay guide](https://oddsindex.com/guides/correlated-parlay-guide)).
- [Claim, Snippet] A double-double is an AND condition; low minutes, blowouts, and foul trouble suppress both legs at once. Among player-games with at least 8 rebounds, about 45% were points-rebounds double-doubles, and the rebounds leg is usually the binding constraint (no methodology given) ([DataStreak](https://thedatastreak.com/insights/bet-nba-double-double-props)).
- [Measured, unreviewed, self-reported] An open-source prop tool computes each stat's probability of reaching 10 from its backtest error table, combines them as independent events, then applies a logistic correction fit on a walk-forward backtest because stats rise and fall together with minutes. The author reports independence alone under-rated triple-doubles by roughly half; on 22,380 backtested games the corrected model's Brier score was 0.0047 vs 0.0054 for a player's last-10-game rate for triple-doubles ([GitHub: JoshuaDHaber PR #16](https://github.com/JoshuaDHaber/NFL-Prop-Predictor/pull/16)).
- [Measured, unreviewed, self-reported] Another open-source tool prices same-game legs with a Gaussian copula: 302 pairwise correlations, each a maximum-likelihood fit to the four joint outcomes at that pair's own lines, shrunk by jackknife error, then projected to the nearest positive-definite matrix (least eigenvalue 0.029), fit on 2023 to 2025 (5,276 games). On 2025-26 DraftKings play-in and playoff lines, combinations it rated more likely hit 26.00% of the time vs 26.17% predicted by the copula and 23.79% by independence ([GitHub: DEMON-X13 PR #4](https://github.com/DEMON-X13/nba-hub/pull/4)).
- [Claim, Snippet] A prediction-model guide frames double-double and triple-double props as binary classification with class imbalance, recommending class weighting, Platt scaling or isotonic calibration, and player-stratified cross-validation ([PredictionEngine](https://predictionengine.app/learn/nba-player-props-guide)).

### Inferences
- Mechanism for the shape: conditional on minutes and pace, rebounds and assists behave like counts of many small-probability events, so Poisson is a reasonable conditional model. Unconditionally, the count is a mixture over the minutes distribution (blowouts, foul trouble, early exits, overtime), which produces variance above the mean, the pattern a negative binomial captures. Points are a sum of 1-, 2-, and 3-point events, so their variance exceeds a Poisson's with the same mean; simulation from attempts and make rates, or a normal or negative binomial approximation fit to residuals, is more appropriate than Poisson for points. This should be checked directly with variance-to-mean ratios on 2023-24 to 2025-26 game logs.
- Tail sensitivity: with a Poisson mean of 9 rebounds, P(10 or more) = 0.413. Holding the mean fixed, extra dispersion generally raises the probability of reaching 10 when the mean is below 10 and lowers it when the mean is above 10, so the dispersion choice matters most for players whose means sit a few rebounds from the threshold.
- Practical joint-probability recipe for P(rebounds >= 10 and points >= 10): (1) simulate minutes from a scenario mixture; (2) simulate pace; (3) draw each stat from its conditional distribution given minutes and pace, using per-minute rates from Section 2; (4) add residual correlation between stats (estimated from historical residuals after removing the minutes effect) with a Gaussian copula on the marginal CDFs; (5) count the share of simulations that meet both thresholds; (6) calibrate against historical outcomes with isotonic or Platt scaling and score with Brier or log loss. Shared minutes alone induce positive dependence, so independence will understate the joint probability.
- Because books set lines near medians, a projection that outputs means will lean to overs on right-skewed counts unless converted to a full distribution.

### Gaps
- No published per-stat dispersion estimates for player rebounds, assists, or points at the game level; no published within-player PTS-REB-AST correlation matrix (by position or role). Both are computable from free game logs and should be estimated in-house.
- The open-source backtests above are self-reported and unreviewed.
- No Pinnacle, Unabated, or Rufus Peabody write-up on pricing double-doubles specifically was found.

## 6. Which NBA APIs or datasets do these studies use (stats.nba.com via nba_api, Basketball Reference, pbpstats, Kaggle)?

### Takeaway
Public projection work draws on NBA.com stats (usually through the nba_api Python client), Basketball-Reference, pbpstats.com, and Kaggle dumps. nba_api is free and MIT-licensed but stats.nba.com throttles rapid requests and often does not respond to cloud IP ranges. Sports Reference caps automated access at 20 requests per minute for Basketball-Reference and has no API.

### Cited Findings
- [Claim] nba_api: community-maintained Python client for NBA.com APIs, MIT-licensed, Python 3.10+, no API key; stats endpoints cover player and team stats, box scores, game logs, shot charts, play-by-play, and standings (WNBA and G League via league_id); live endpoints (cdn.nba.com) include ScoreBoard, BoxScore(game_id), PlayByPlay(game_id), and Odds. Default timeout 30 seconds; proxy and header options. stats.nba.com "throttles rapid requests and often doesn't respond to many cloud IP ranges, including AWS, GCP, Azure, and Colab"; data are subject to NBA.com Terms of Use ([GitHub: swar/nba_api](https://github.com/swar/nba_api)).
- [Claim] Sports Reference bot policy (dated October 26, 2022, updated May 29, 2024): no more than 20 requests per minute on its non-FBref sites (10 for FBref and Stathead), regardless of bot type or pages; violators' sessions are jailed for up to a day; no API ("not our business model"); most data are bought from third parties ([Sports Reference](https://www.sports-reference.com/bot-traffic.html)).
- [Claim] DARKO's data sources: Basketball-Reference.com, NBA.com, tracking data, play-by-play, game-level information, and pbpstats.com (built by Darryl Blackport); 787,782 player games since 1996-97 ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)).
- [Claim] The 2025-26 back-to-back analysis pulled game-level data from NBA.com via nba_api ([NBAAnalytic](https://nbaanalytic.com/articles/rest-back-to-backs-load-management.html)).
- [Claim] The UBC minutes model used the Kaggle "NBA Enhanced Box Score and Standings" dataset (Paul Rossotti; sourced from xmlstats APIs; 2012 to 2018; 151,493 player-game rows) ([GitHub: UBC-MDS](https://github.com/UBC-MDS/NBA-Minutes-Predictor)).
- [Claim] van Bommel and Bornn used ESPN.com box scores and optical tracking data (1,227 games, 2015-16) ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1602.08754)). Statathlon used Basketball-Reference game logs ([Statathlon](https://statathlon.com/evaluating-the-effect-of-mid-season-absences/)). A 2026 GitHub backtest used NBA.com historical daily lineups to identify actual starters ([GitHub: Eth91 PR #114](https://github.com/Eth91/tennis-odds-collector/pull/114)).

### Inferences
- A daily pipeline can be built entirely from nba_api (game logs, advanced and tracking box scores, play-by-play for on/off splits) plus pbpstats for possession-level on/off data, but it should run from a residential or non-cloud IP or a proxy, with pauses between calls. Basketball-Reference is suited to backfill at under 20 requests per minute.
- NBA.com tracking data include potential assists and rebound chances (van Bommel and Bornn counted 82,493 potential assists from tracking), which are the natural denominators for opportunity-based rebound and assist models; the exact nba_api endpoint names were not verified in this session.

### Gaps
- pbpstats.com API terms and rate limits, the official NBA injury report format and timing, and current Kaggle NBA datasets (beyond the 2012 to 2018 set) were not reviewed.
- No source documented which endpoints commercial projection systems use for minutes or lineups.
